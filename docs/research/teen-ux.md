# Teen CGM dashboard: UX research

Research notes for a CGM dashboard used by a teenager with type 1 diabetes on a Tandem pump with Control-IQ, reading glucose in mmol/L. The teen uses the dashboard on a phone. Its suggestions should help the teen and a parent prepare questions for the care team about pump settings. The dashboard does not change pump settings itself.

Sources were gathered from the web in October 2026. Where only an abstract, a vendor page or a secondary summary was available, the text says so. Glucose values in mg/dL from US sources are converted at 18 mg/dL per mmol/L.

## 1. Adolescents, data and emotion

### Clinical targets the teen will see

The 2019 International Consensus on Time in Range sets the standard CGM metrics and the five-band split used by every major report: very low below 3.0 mmol/L, low 3.0 to 3.8, in range 3.9 to 10.0, high 10.1 to 13.9, very high above 13.9. Targets for most people with type 1 diabetes are more than 70% in range, under 4% below 3.9, under 1% below 3.0, under 25% above 10.0 and under 5% above 13.9. The panel recommends 14 days of data with at least 70% sensor wear, and suggests expressing goals as time per day (for example, under 1 hour per day below range) because that is more motivating than percentages. It also notes that terms like "glucose variability" are unfamiliar to many people with diabetes. ([Battelino et al. 2019, Diabetes Care](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6973648/))

The ISPAD 2022 pediatric guideline adopts the same CGM targets for children, adolescents and young people under 25 (more than 70% at 3.9 to 10 mmol/L, under 4% below 3.9, under 1% below 3.0, under 25% above 10, under 5% above 13.9, CV at or below 36%). It says reducing time below 3.0 mmol/L matters most, and that less strict goals can apply where chasing the target would harm overall wellbeing. ([ISPAD 2022, de Bock et al.](https://www.ispad.org/asset/216A2927%2D7CBC%2D4F56%2DB82257DF47E6C61C)) A 2024 ISPAD update proposes an HbA1c target of 6.5% or lower for young people with CGM and automated insulin delivery, where it does not add burden that harms quality of life. ([ISPAD 2024 targets, Karger](https://karger.com/hrp/article/97/6/546/918393))

### Diabetes distress and burnout

Diabetes distress is common in this age group. In the Diabetes MILES Youth Australia survey (537 adolescents, mean age 16, 62% girls), PAID-T cut points of under 70, 70 to 90 and over 90 separated none-to-mild, moderate and high distress. Moderate distress affected 18% and high distress 36%. Depressive symptoms, self-reported HbA1c and self-monitoring frequency all differed across distress levels. ([Hagger et al. 2017, Diabetes Care, record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1271374-cut-points-for-identifying-clinically-significant-diabetes-distress-in-adolescents-with-type-1-diabetes-using-the-paid-t--results-from-diabetes-miles-youth-australia); [summary with percentages, The Limbic](https://thelimbic.com/endocrinology/diabetes-stress-negatively-impacts-hba1c/))

Constant data adds its own load. A UK clinical review defines alarm fatigue as reduced response to real alarms after frequent false or unnecessary ones, and argues that always-available data can raise anxiety. ([Stamford, Diabetes on the Net](https://diabetesonthenet.com/wp-content/uploads/Stamford-FINAL.pdf)) A 2023 Endocrine Society presentation of settings from 150 youths found alert thresholds often far from recommended values, which can produce excess alarms. ([MDedge summary](https://mdedge.com/content/cgm-alarm-fatigue-youth))

### Do teens look at their data?

In a single-clinic survey of 96 adolescents aged 12 to 18, 53% said they review glucose data to think about insulin dose changes, and 88% of those did so regularly. Reviewers had lower HbA1c (median 52 vs 65 mmol/mol) and higher time in range (57.5% vs 45.9%). The study is cross-sectional, so the direction of cause is unknown, and it did not ask non-reviewers why they did not review. ([Chenoweth et al. 2024, Pediatric Diabetes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12017010/))

In a 21-person cognitive interview study of AGP plus pump reports, all participants preferred colored graphs over numbers, most preferred phone or watch interfaces, and 75% had no home computer. 95% spotted trends and highs, but only 57% found the report easy to understand, mainly because of the insulin graphs. After explanation, 73% understood the bolus graph, and only 38% could teach it back with specific advice. ([Mullen, Bergenstal, Johnson 2020, Diabetes abstract](https://www.healthpartners.com/knowledgeexchange/display/document-rn21174))

A study of reflection tools with patients, caregivers and clinicians found that users wanted specific summary metrics, low-effort tools, visuals paired with text explanations, concise but adjustable views, and actionable insights. 84% found a day-by-hour matrix view useful for spotting patterns tied to times and days. ([Prioleau, Sabharwal, Vasudevan 2020, arXiv/Pervasive Health](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12761))

### What motivates engagement

Focus groups with teens aged 10 to 15 and parents, run to design the MyT1DHero app, named four themes: diabetes is unpredictable, communication between parent and teen is often negative and frustrated, motivations to use an app, and feedback on the app. Participants said customization, interactivity and tangible rewards were needed for continued use. ([Holtz et al. 2017, JMIR mHealth](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2017/4/e53/)) The 12-week single-arm pilot (25 families analyzed) found improved adherence and quality of life. Changes in HbA1c and family conflict were in the expected direction but not significant, and more app use was associated with more HbA1c improvement. ([Holtz et al. 2021, JMIR mHealth](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2021/9/e23916/PDF))

Self-determination theory work points to autonomy support. In 135 adolescents, autonomy-supportive communication from providers was associated with adherence, and adherence was highest when both providers and parents were seen as supportive. A two-year study of 289 adolescents found provider autonomy support predicted later self-efficacy and autonomous self-regulation. These studies are mostly correlational. ([PMC8008789](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8008789); [Austin et al. 2013](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/2013_AustinGuaySenecalFernetNouwen.pdf))

In the FLEX trial (122 adolescents aged 13 to 16), counseling sessions where the teen's own goals combined glucose monitoring, CGM and insulin dosing were associated with a 0.4% lower HbA1c at the end of the intervention. Goals covering only glucose checks or only dosing showed no association. This is an observational analysis inside a trial. ([Sarteau et al. 2020, Pediatric Diabetes](https://diabetescenters.org/bibcite/reference/4123))

### Language

The 2017 ADA and AADE (now ADCES) consensus statement asks for language that is neutral, nonjudgmental and based on facts, actions or physiology, and free from stigma. It suggests replacing "control" with words like "manage", and its example sentence uses "checking blood glucose levels". ([Dickinson et al. 2017, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29042412/); [Healio summary](https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20171019/consensus-statement-diabetes-language-matters))

The diaTribe Foundation's dStigmatize guide gives concrete swaps. Instead of "good", "bad" or "poor" glucose, use exact numbers or describe choices. Use "high glucose levels", "low glucose levels" and "within target range" or "outside of target range". Replace "test" with "check" or "monitor", and for CGM use "read" or "reading". Avoid "control", "uncontrolled", "compliance" and "adherence", and describe the specific behavior instead. ([dStigmatize language guide](https://dstigmatize.org/language-guide/))

NHS England's Language Matters (second edition) says negative language matters even more with children and young people, who are still forming their identity. It asks for language that is free from judgment and scolding, collaborative rather than authoritarian, and it lists models such as clear ("your HbA1c this time is higher than the recommended range"), encouraging ("I can see the effort you're putting in") and person-centred ("what thoughts have you had yourself about your recent glucose levels?"). It names two pediatric examples: clinic posters at Leeds Children's Hospital and the Ormskirk HbA1c and time-in-range scale for non-judgmental, solution-focused discussion. ([NHS England, Language Matters PDF](https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/language-matters-language-and-diabetes-v2.pdf))

A London pediatric clinic trained staff to stop calling readings "good" or "bad", and also moved away from "high" and "low" toward "above or below target range". Children, young people and staff agreed that "good" and "bad" labels hurt how they perceived clinic outcomes. The abstract gives no sample size. ([Ali et al., BSPED 2022 poster](https://www.endocrine-abstracts.org/ea/0085/abstracts/poster-presentations/diabetes-1/ea0085p21/))

Tidepool's design team wanted non-emotional icons because strong emotional reactions to data make it harder to learn from what happened. Testers found red and green "judgmental and alarming". ([Tidepool blog](https://www.tidepool.org/blog/colors-shapes-and-blood-sugars-a-look-into-our-process))

### Family conflict around data

Lawton et al. interviewed 18 adolescents aged 11 to 18 and 19 parents six months after starting the CamAPS FX closed loop. Parents of 11 to 13 year olds acted on near real-time data. Parents of 14 to 16 year olds mostly practiced "watchful waiting" and stepped in only when a pattern appeared. Some young people aged 17 and over did not share data at all. Unwelcome messages caused annoyance, and one teen turned off a parent's notifications. Parents could see meal and carb entries, which some expected to become a privacy issue. Participants recommended clear ground rules on when and how shared data is used, and the authors recommend designs that allow data use to change over time. ([Lawton et al. 2021, Diabetes Technology and Therapeutics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8252900/))

Fremont and Miller interviewed 43 parents and 41 youth (ages 8 to 17) two months after starting CGM. Remote monitoring gave peace of mind, but excessive texting caused conflict, and parents worried about over-involvement. Families solved this by setting boundaries, waiting before intervening and trusting the youth. The authors recommend iterative family discussions about boundaries. ([Fremont and Miller 2021, Journal of Pediatric Psychology](https://academic.oup.com/jpepsy/article/46/10/1276/6333324))

The CloudConnect trial sent weekly automated dosing advice to teens and parents. HbA1c and time in range did not differ from usual care. Parents reported less conflict and teens did not, and both parents and teens reported a more negative communication tone. ([De Boer et al. 2023, Diabetes Therapy, via Drug Topics](https://www.drugtopics.com/view/pediatric-t1d-management-compliance-system-evaluated-in-study))

Broader studies link family conflict to poorer self-care and higher HbA1c, and link parental monitoring to better self-care. ([PMC3811134](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3811134)) Teens may experience constant supervision as intrusive, and perceived over-involvement has been associated with poorer outcomes. ([PMC5446384](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5446384))

## 2. How existing tools show CGM data

### The AGP standard

The International Diabetes Center's AGP report has three parts: a five-band time-in-ranges bar with glucose metrics (average glucose, GMI, CV, time CGM active), the AGP curve, and daily glucose profiles. The AGP curve shows the median (50%) line, the 25% to 75% interquartile band where half of all readings fall, and the 5% and 95% lines, as if all days happened in one day. The curve's colors match the bar: green inside 3.9 to 10.0 mmol/L and red below 3.9. The report states "each 1% time in range = about 15 minutes per day" and "each 5% increase in the target range is clinically beneficial". ([IDC/HealthPartners AGP guide PDF](https://www.healthpartners.com/institute/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Ambulatory-Glucose-Profile-Report-Overview.pdf))

The consensus figure uses green for in range, two shades of red for low and very low, and two shades of yellow and orange for high and very high. ([Battelino et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6973648/)) A Novo Nordisk education page describes the same mapping: red and dark red below range, yellow and orange above. ([NovoMedLink](https://www.novomedlink.com/diabetes/hcp-education/clinical/time-in-range/clinical-use/understand-ambulatory-glucose-profile.html))

The Mullen abstract above suggests people read the CGM parts of the AGP well and struggle with the insulin graphs. ([HealthPartners abstract](https://www.healthpartners.com/knowledgeexchange/display/document-rn21174))

### Dexcom Clarity

Clarity's Overlay report puts each day's trace on one weekly view to show frequency, duration and severity of highs and lows. Clarity defines a pattern as highs or lows at the same time of day on several days, and shows them as named callouts (for example, nighttime highs between two clock times). ([Dexcom Clarity, Australia](https://dexcom.com/en-au/clarity-australia); [Clarity patient handout PDF](https://www.teladochealth.com/content/dam/tdh-www/us/en/documents/helpcenter/LBL013937-Rev-16-Dexcom-CLARITY-Patient-Sharing-Handout.pdf)) The mobile app has opt-in notifications: a daily time-in-range goal (default 70%, user-adjustable), a "Best Day" notice for the day with most time in range in the past week, and a weekly summary that compares the week with the previous one and lists high and low patterns. ([diaTribe on Clarity goals and Best Day](https://diatribe.org/dexcom-clarity-app-updates-time-range-goals-best-day-and-estimated-a1c); [diaTribe on weekly notifications](https://diatribe.org/dexcom-upgrades-clarity-mobile-app-add-weekly-notifications))

### Tandem Source and Sugarmate

Tandem Source is Tandem's web platform. Its reports include an overview, a daily timeline with glucose, basal delivery and boluses, and a pump settings report showing personal profiles. Data from the t:slim X2 uploads hourly through the mobile app. ([Tandem Source](https://www.tandemdiabetes.com/en-ca/products/software-apps/tandem-source)) A pediatric clinic worksheet for Control-IQ (PANTHER program) uses Dashboard, Therapy Timeline, CGM Hourly and Device Settings reports over two weeks and a three-step review: patterns, then reasons, then plan. ([PANTHER Control-IQ worksheet PDF](https://cdn.baptistjax.com/image/upload/v1711641989/WolfsonChildrens/Services/Endo/PANTHER_Control-IQ_0623.pdf)) Its contents are summarized in section 5 because they shape the suggestion feature.

Tandem bought Sugarmate in 2020. ([Tandem press release](https://www.tandemdiabetes.com/blog/post/tandem-diabetes-care-acquires-sugarmate-a-popular-mobile-app-for-people-with-diabetes-who-use-insulin)) Sugarmate offers more than 30 statistics over a chosen date range, percentage in range, low and high, emailed spreadsheet reports, an activity feed and a nightstand mode. ([Dexcom Sugarmate page](https://www.dexcom.com/en-gb/sugarmate); [Sugarmate by Tandem](https://www.tandemdiabetes.com/products/software-apps/sugarmate))

### Tidepool

Tidepool's web app has Basics, Daily, Trends and Device Settings views. Daily splits one day into lanes for notes, glucose, bolus and carbs, and basal. ([Tidepool viz feature overview](https://unpkg.com/@tidepool/viz@0.8.6/web/docs/FeatureOverview.html)) In the CGM Trends view, each colored box shows average glucose for a 30-minute segment over the reporting period (default 2 weeks), and days of the week can be included or excluded. ([Tidepool support: Trends](https://support.tidepool.org/hc/en-us/articles/360029760391-Viewing-Your-Data-Trends)) Tidepool uses the same five thresholds for everyone. ([Tidepool summary data docs](https://dev-docs.tidepool.org/docs/summary-data.md)) Its design team chose five categories, avoided red and green and avoided relying on color alone (for color-blind users and black-and-white printing), rejected triangles because people read them as trend arrows, and settled on circles that differ by fill, stroke and color, after more than 80 icon iterations and more than 30 interviews. ([Tidepool blog](https://www.tidepool.org/blog/colors-shapes-and-blood-sugars-a-look-into-our-process))

### Glooko

Glooko's summary view is an at-a-glance overview with five tiers (very low, low, target, high, very high) following the consensus, a default range of 70 to 180 mg/dL (3.9 to 10.0 mmol/L) that the patient or provider can change, and statistics including average, standard deviation, median and GMI. ([Glooko support](https://support.glooko.com/hc/articles/4402092408467)) Its report views include Daily Overview, Calendar, Logbook, Week View and Graphs, with color-coded readings and dotted target lines. ([Glooko report legend](https://support.glooko.com/hc/en-us/articles/4402435269907-Glooko-Report-Legend-Reference-Guide))

### Nightscout

Nightscout reports include Day to Day (glucose per day with selected treatments), Distribution (time in range plus estimated HbA1c and other statistics), Hourly Stats (box plot per hour) and a Percentile chart. ([Nightscout docs: reports](https://nightscout.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nightscout/reports.html); [Nightscout clinician guide](https://nightscout.readthedocs.io/en/latest/clinicians/reports.html))

### Gluroo

Gluroo is built around a group chat (GluCrew) where each message carries the glucose value at that moment. Parents, school nurses, grandparents and others can be invited, meals can be logged from photos with AI carb estimates, and a low alert goes to the person with diabetes first and to the rest of the group only if it is not addressed within a few minutes. These claims come from Gluroo's own site. ([Gluroo](https://gluroo.com); [Gluroo blog on GluCrew](https://gluroo.com/blog/glucrew/how-to-support-multiple-children-with-diabetes/))

### mySugr

mySugr uses a "diabetes monster" that the user tames by earning points, and challenges that add rewards. Points come mainly from logging, and at least one challenge ("No Hitter") rewards keeping glucose in range. Point values come from articles of different ages and may have changed. ([mySugr blog: monster and points](https://mysugr.com/en/blog/story-behind-diabetes-monster-and-point-system); [Accu-Chek HCP guide](https://www.accu-chek.co.uk/hcp/resources/three-ways-boost-your-patients-motivation-mysugr-app))

### Heatmaps and long-term views

PixelGrid lays CGM data in a matrix of days by hours, and 84% of evaluators found it useful for time- and day-specific patterns. ([Prioleau et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12761)) Work from the same lab on months-long data found that views by month, weekday and hour helped people notice trends and reflect on causes. ([Dartmouth AH Lab](https://ah-lab.cs.dartmouth.edu/publications/empowering-self-management-of-diabetes-through-long-term-wearable-data-and-seasonal-visualizations)) Rodbard proposed stacked bars of glucose distribution by time of day, meal or weekday and argued readers learn them with little training, as a methods proposal rather than a usability test. ([Rodbard 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2787039))

### Weather and score metaphors

I found no published evaluation of "glucose weather" or single daily "glucose score" metaphors for teens. Forecast features exist in adult products (Medtronic Sugar.IQ low prediction, Accu-Chek SmartGuide Predict for adults 18 and over), but not as weather metaphors. ([Sugar.IQ guide](https://www.medtronicdiabetes.com/sites/default/files/library/download-library/user-guides/Sugar_IQv3.pdf); [Accu-Chek SmartGuide Predict](https://apps.apple.com/cz/app/accu-chek-smartguide-predict/id6504021989)) The closest validated composite is the Glycemia Risk Index (GRI). It has a hypoglycemia and a hyperglycemia component, weights very low and very high more heavily, and its authors recommend showing the two components separately. A combined GRI correlated with clinician rankings at r = 0.95, but it was built on adult data (225 adults, 330 clinicians). ([Klonoff et al. 2022, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35348391/); [ADCES danatech on GRI](https://adces.org/education/danatech/glucose-monitoring/continuous-glucose-monitors-(cgm)/glycemic-metrics--an-overview/using-gri-to-deepen-glycemic-data-interpretation))

## 3. Gamification and positive feedback

What the evidence supports:

- Rewards change behavior in the short term. In a 2012 pilot app for adolescents, gamified rewards raised daily glucose checks from 2.4 to 3.6, with high satisfaction, in a small sample. ([JMIR 2012 pilot](https://jmir.org/2012/3/e70/PDF))
- Rewards for behavior do not automatically change glucose. In the BE IN CONTROL trial (90 people aged 14 to 20), daily financial incentives raised monitoring adherence to about 50% versus 18.9% in controls, but glycemic control did not significantly improve. ([Wong et al. 2017, JAMA Pediatrics, CHIBE](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/effect-of-financial-incentives-on-glucose-monitoring-adherence-and-glycemic-control-among-adolescents-and-young-adults-with-type-1-diabetes-a-randomized-clinical-trial/); [trial registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02568501))
- Teens ask for customization, interactivity and tangible rewards. ([Holtz et al. 2017](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2017/4/e53/))
- Goals teens set themselves, combining CGM and dosing, are associated with lower HbA1c. ([Sarteau et al. 2020](https://diabetescenters.org/bibcite/reference/4123))
- Clarity already uses opt-in goal and Best Day notifications with a user-adjustable goal. ([diaTribe](https://diatribe.org/dexcom-clarity-app-updates-time-range-goals-best-day-and-estimated-a1c))

What backfires:

- Broken streaks. Across seven experiments, people who saw a broken streak were less likely to continue than people with the same actual history shown as unbroken. The effect was stronger when people blamed themselves and weaker when the streak could be repaired. ([Silverman and Barasch 2023, Journal of Consumer Research](https://udspace.udel.edu/handle/19716/34160); [CU Boulder summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions))
- Streaks also have a mechanical problem: habit research found that missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation, while a streak counter resets to zero. ([Lally et al. 2010, Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/ejsp.674))
- Automated advice delivered to both teen and parent made the communication tone more negative in CloudConnect. ([Drug Topics](https://www.drugtopics.com/view/pediatric-t1d-management-compliance-system-evaluated-in-study))
- Value labels on numbers ("good", "bad") hurt how young people perceive outcomes. ([Ali et al. 2022](https://www.endocrine-abstracts.org/ea/0085/abstracts/poster-presentations/diabetes-1/ea0085p21/))

Inference, not directly tested in teens: rewarding glucose outcomes (for example, mySugr's "No Hitter" in-range challenge) ties praise to numbers that sensor errors, growth, illness and hormones also move. That conflicts with the language guidance to describe facts and behaviors rather than grade results. Rewards tied to things the teen controls (sensor wear, Control-IQ time active, a pre-meal bolus the teen chose to track) avoid that conflict. Comparison against the teen's own recent baseline also fits the AGP's "each 5% increase is clinically beneficial" framing better than an all-or-nothing 70% bar. ([AGP guide](https://www.healthpartners.com/institute/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Ambulatory-Glucose-Profile-Report-Overview.pdf))

## 4. Accessibility, color and layout

### Color rules

WCAG 1.4.1 says color must not be the only way to convey information. WCAG 1.4.11 asks for at least 3:1 contrast against adjacent colors for graphical objects needed to understand content, such as chart segments. ([W3C WCAG 2.2 Understanding docs](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/understanding/); [Queensland Health design system summary](https://www.design-system.health.qld.gov.au/styles/colour/accessibility); [digitala11y on 1.4.11](https://www.digitala11y.com/understanding-sc-1-4-11-non-text-contrast/))

The AGP palette (green in range, red below, yellow and orange above) puts red next to green, the pair most affected by red-green color vision deficiency. Tidepool notes over 10% of men have trouble telling red and green apart and chose not to rely on color alone. ([Tidepool blog](https://www.tidepool.org/blog/colors-shapes-and-blood-sugars-a-look-into-our-process)) Red-green deficiency is much rarer in girls and women, but a parent or a member of the care team may be affected, so the design should hold up for every viewer. Okabe and Ito's color universal design palette is built to stay distinguishable under the three common forms of color blindness, and the authors also recommend redundant shapes, positions and line styles. ([Okabe and Ito palette, NYU summary](https://siegal.bio.nyu.edu/color-palette/); [Ichihara et al. 2008](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/ichihara_etal_2008.pdf))

The practical approach is to keep the AGP hue families, because the care team reads those every visit, and add redundancy:

- Position: lows always sit below the in-range segment and highs above it, in bars and in charts, as on the AGP.
- Lightness: the "very" bands are darker than their neighbors in both themes, so they separate even when hue does not.
- Text: every band carries a label and a value in hours and minutes.
- Pattern: optional hatching on low bands for print and for color-blind viewers.

Candidate tokens, checked with a script for WCAG contrast against the surface and for color difference under simulated deuteranopia, protanopia and tritanopia (Machado et al. 2009 matrices, CIELAB delta E 1976). These values are a starting point to verify in the real UI, not a validated standard. ([Machado et al. 2009 CVD simulation](https://www.inf.ufrgs.br/~oliveira/pubs_files/CVD_Simulation/CVD_Simulation.html))

| Band (mmol/L) | Light mode | Contrast on #FFFFFF | Dark mode | Contrast on #121212 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Very low, below 3.0 | #8E1B2E | 8.95 | #E8475F | 4.90 |
| Low, 3.0 to 3.8 | #D6404E | 4.47 | #FF99A3 | 9.22 |
| In range, 3.9 to 10.0 | #1E8A5A | 4.34 | #3FB97F | 7.54 |
| High, 10.1 to 13.9 | #B07A00 | 3.73 | #F2C14E | 11.16 |
| Very high, above 13.9 | #A6470A | 5.95 | #FF8A3D | 7.99 |

All pairs stayed at delta E 9 or more under each simulated deficiency. The closest pairs were high and very high (light mode, about 15 under deuteranopia) and high and very high (dark mode, about 9 under deuteranopia), which is why labels and position must carry the meaning too.

### Dark mode

Material Design's dark theme guidance recommends dark grey surfaces (#121212 baseline) rather than pure black, and desaturated colors, because saturated colors vibrate visually on dark backgrounds and can fail 4.5:1 for text. Elevation is shown with lighter surfaces rather than shadows. ([Material Design dark theme](https://m2.material.io/design/color/dark-theme)) The dark-mode tokens above are lighter and less saturated than the light-mode ones, and the "very" bands stay darker than their neighbors in both themes. Test both themes under color-blindness simulation.

### Phone-first layout

People in the Mullen study preferred phone or watch interfaces and checked their phone or CGM many times a day. ([HealthPartners abstract](https://www.healthpartners.com/knowledgeexchange/display/document-rn21174)) The reflection-tool study asked for concise views that can be expanded, and visuals paired with plain text. ([Prioleau et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12761)) On a phone this means one idea per card, a stacked vertical layout, a single caption sentence under each chart, and drill-down for detail rather than dense multi-lane charts.

## 5. Turning patterns into questions for the care team

The PANTHER worksheet for Control-IQ on the t:slim X2 (dated June 2023) gives a clinician workflow that maps well to dashboard suggestions. Key facts it states: Control-IQ aims for 112.5 to 160 mg/dL (6.3 to 8.9 mmol/L); Sleep Activity narrows the target to 112.5 to 120 mg/dL (6.3 to 6.7 mmol/L) and stops automatic correction boluses overnight; Exercise Activity targets 140 to 160 mg/dL (7.8 to 8.9 mmol/L); automatic corrections can occur up to once an hour when glucose is predicted above 180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L) and use 60% of the programmed correction factor. Users and clinicians can change basal rates, carb ratios and correction factors, but not the 110 mg/dL (6.1 mmol/L) correction target or the 5-hour active insulin time while Control-IQ is on. ([PANTHER Control-IQ worksheet](https://cdn.baptistjax.com/image/upload/v1711641989/WolfsonChildrens/Services/Endo/PANTHER_Control-IQ_0623.pdf)) A newer Tandem pump or app version may differ, so the dashboard should read these values from the device data rather than hard-code them.

The worksheet's checks, in order:

1. System use first: Control-IQ in use more than 90% of the time, CGM inactive less than 10%, sleep schedule averaging 6 hours or more per day.
2. Meal boluses: if food boluses are under 50% of total insulin, assess missed meal boluses or carb ratios.
3. Targets: if time below range is over 4%, work on lows first; otherwise work on highs.
4. Timing: is the pattern overnight or fasting, 1 to 3 hours after meals, a low after a high, a high after a low, around exercise, or 1 to 3 hours after a correction?
5. Plan: each timing maps to a setting (basal for overnight, carb ratio for after meals, correction factor for after corrections, Exercise Activity for exercise lows) or to a behavior (bolus before eating, treat lows with 5 to 10 g and wait 15 minutes).

The dashboard can run steps 1 to 4 on the teen's data and phrase step 5 as a question for the care team, without dose numbers. Example: "Most of your highs in the last 2 weeks started 1 to 3 hours after dinner. Question for clinic: could the dinner carb ratio be looked at?" This keeps decisions with the care team, follows the "clear and collaborative" language model ([NHS Language Matters](https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/language-matters-language-and-diabetes-v2.pdf)), and avoids the CloudConnect pattern of automatic dosing advice sent to both teen and parent. ([Drug Topics](https://www.drugtopics.com/view/pediatric-t1d-management-compliance-system-evaluated-in-study))

## Design principles

1. Describe, never grade. Use "in range", "high", "low", "above target", "below target", numbers and time. Never "good", "bad", "poor", "control", "compliance", "failed", or "test". Use "reading" for CGM values and "check" for fingersticks. ([dStigmatize](https://dstigmatize.org/language-guide/); [Ali et al. 2022](https://www.endocrine-abstracts.org/ea/0085/abstracts/poster-presentations/diabetes-1/ea0085p21/))
2. Use the consensus bands and thresholds in mmol/L, and show time as hours and minutes per day next to percentages. ([Battelino et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6973648/); [AGP guide](https://www.healthpartners.com/institute/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Ambulatory-Glucose-Profile-Report-Overview.pdf))
3. Keep the AGP hue families for continuity with clinic reports, and never rely on color alone: add position, labels, lightness steps and optional patterns. Meet 3:1 non-text contrast in both themes. ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/understanding/); [Tidepool](https://www.tidepool.org/blog/colors-shapes-and-blood-sugars-a-look-into-our-process))
4. Pair every chart with one plain sentence that says what it shows. ([Prioleau et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12761))
5. Lead with CGM views. Keep insulin detail behind a tap, because people understand insulin graphs least. ([Mullen et al. 2020](https://www.healthpartners.com/knowledgeexchange/display/document-rn21174))
6. Prioritize lows. When time below 3.9 is over 4%, the lows card comes first and the suggestion engine works on lows before highs. ([ISPAD 2022](https://www.ispad.org/asset/216A2927%2D7CBC%2D4F56%2DB82257DF47E6C61C); [PANTHER](https://cdn.baptistjax.com/image/upload/v1711641989/WolfsonChildrens/Services/Endo/PANTHER_Control-IQ_0623.pdf))
7. Suggestions are questions for the care team, not dose instructions, and the teen sees them before anyone else does. ([De Boer et al. 2023](https://www.drugtopics.com/view/pediatric-t1d-management-compliance-system-evaluated-in-study))
8. The teen owns sharing. The teen chooses which cards the parent sees and can change that over time. The app prompts the family to agree on ground rules when sharing is set up. ([Lawton et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8252900/); [Fremont and Miller 2021](https://academic.oup.com/jpepsy/article/46/10/1276/6333324))
9. Goals belong to the teen. The teen picks a goal and can change it, the default compares against the teen's own recent weeks, and the goal can be about a behavior. ([Sarteau et al. 2020](https://diabetescenters.org/bibcite/reference/4123); [PMC8008789](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8008789))
10. No streak counters that reset to zero. Count "days this week" or "weeks in a row with at least N days" with grace for missed days and sensor gaps, and never notify about a broken run. ([Silverman and Barasch 2023](https://udspace.udel.edu/handle/19716/34160); [Lally et al. 2010](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/ejsp.674))
11. Reward what the teen controls (sensor wear, Control-IQ active, a bolus habit the teen chose), and describe glucose outcomes as information.
12. Show data sufficiency. If sensor wear is under 70% of 14 days, say the summary may be less reliable instead of computing a confident score. ([Battelino et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6973648/))
13. Opt-in, low-frequency notifications. Weekly by default, nothing that adds to existing CGM alarms. ([Stamford](https://diabetesonthenet.com/wp-content/uploads/Stamford-FINAL.pdf); [Clarity notifications](https://diatribe.org/dexcom-upgrades-clarity-mobile-app-add-weekly-notifications))
14. Let the teen personalize the look (theme, card order, which cards show). ([Holtz et al. 2017](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2017/4/e53/))

## Dashboard views ranked by usefulness

1. Time in ranges card (this week vs last week). One horizontal five-band bar for the last 7 days, with a thinner bar for the week before. Labels in hours and minutes per day ("In range 17 h 10 min"). A single sentence such as "About 40 minutes more in range per day than last week." This is the most standard and most quickly read view, it matches the clinic AGP, and the week-over-week comparison is how Clarity frames its weekly summary. ([AGP guide](https://www.healthpartners.com/institute/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Ambulatory-Glucose-Profile-Report-Overview.pdf); [Clarity weekly](https://diatribe.org/dexcom-upgrades-clarity-mobile-app-add-weekly-notifications))
2. Typical day (simplified AGP). 14-day median line with the 25 to 75% band shaded and the 3.9 to 10.0 target band marked, 5 to 95% lines behind a toggle. Caption names the time window that stands out ("Readings are usually highest between 21:00 and 23:00"). Everyone in the cognitive interview study spotted trends on this kind of view. ([Mullen et al. 2020](https://www.healthpartners.com/knowledgeexchange/display/document-rn21174))
3. Patterns and questions for clinic. Up to three cards from the PANTHER-style checks: the pattern, when it happens, how many days of 14, and one question for the care team. Lows first when time below range is over 4%. The teen can mark a question "save for clinic". ([PANTHER](https://cdn.baptistjax.com/image/upload/v1711641989/WolfsonChildrens/Services/Endo/PANTHER_Control-IQ_0623.pdf); [Clarity patterns](https://www.teladochealth.com/content/dam/tdh-www/us/en/documents/helpcenter/LBL013937-Rev-16-Dexcom-CLARITY-Patient-Sharing-Handout.pdf))
4. Weekly wins and the teen's goal. Progress toward a goal the teen set, plus two or three factual wins from the week (most in-range day, sensor wear %, Control-IQ active %, fewer lows than last week). Counts days, not streaks. ([Holtz et al. 2017](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2017/4/e53/); [Silverman and Barasch 2023](https://udspace.udel.edu/handle/19716/34160))
5. Hour by weekday heatmap. Rows for Monday to Sunday, columns for hours, cells colored by the band the median falls in, with the band name on tap. Useful for school-day vs weekend differences. ([Prioleau et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12761); [Tidepool Trends](https://support.tidepool.org/hc/en-us/articles/360029760391-Viewing-Your-Data-Trends))
6. Day detail. One day's trace with the target band, and below it, behind a tap, basal, boluses, carbs and Sleep or Exercise Activity. Used to look into a specific day the teen remembers. ([Tidepool Daily](https://unpkg.com/@tidepool/viz@0.8.6/web/docs/FeatureOverview.html); [Tandem Source daily timeline](https://www.tandemdiabetes.com/en-ca/products/software-apps/tandem-source))
7. System check. CGM active %, Control-IQ in use %, average nightly sleep schedule hours, food boluses per day. Each with the PANTHER reference value and neutral wording. ([PANTHER](https://cdn.baptistjax.com/image/upload/v1711641989/WolfsonChildrens/Services/Endo/PANTHER_Control-IQ_0623.pdf))
8. Clinic summary to share. A 14-day one-pager in AGP layout plus the teen's saved questions, which the teen can send to the parent or bring to clinic. Sharing is the teen's action. ([AGP guide](https://www.healthpartners.com/institute/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Ambulatory-Glucose-Profile-Report-Overview.pdf); [Lawton et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8252900/))

## Gaps in the evidence

- I found no study that tests weather or score metaphors with teens, or a direct comparison of AGP, overlay and heatmap comprehension in adolescents.
- Several sources were available only as abstracts or summaries: Mullen 2020, Ali 2022, De Boer 2023, Hagger 2017 percentages, and the Clarity feature descriptions.
- Gluroo and mySugr descriptions come from the companies themselves.
- The PANTHER worksheet is a clinic tool from 2023 for the t:slim X2. Current Tandem hardware and software may use different values.
